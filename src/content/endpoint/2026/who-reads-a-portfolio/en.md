## The inevitable question

After investing time in designing, writing, and publishing a portfolio, a difficult question inevitably appears:

Who is going to read all this?

The most honest answer is probably not reassuring. Many recruiters will open the link for only a few seconds. Some will look only at the heading, recent experience, and primary technologies. A technical decision-maker may visit GitHub. Others will never open it.

That raises a reasonable doubt: is building a portfolio worthwhile when we do not know who will read it?

I believe it is, but not only because of the visits it may receive.

A portfolio is not merely a piece of communication aimed outward. It is also a tool for observing oneself professionally from a certain distance.

## A narrative, not merely a list

A CV summarises. LinkedIn classifies. GitHub exposes repositories. A portfolio makes it possible to build a narrative.

It does not merely list jobs, technologies, and studies. It can show how they relate:

what experience I have; which problems I can solve; how I work; what I am building; what I think about the industry; where I want to go next.

A career may include backend development, architecture, integration, technical coordination, and full-stack development. Presented as a list, these elements look like fragments. Within a portfolio, they can form a comprehensible professional identity.

The site acts as an interface between a complex career and someone who does not yet know it.

## The first reader is oneself

While building my portfolio, I realised that many apparently visual decisions were actually decisions about my professional identity.

Which title should I use? Senior Software Engineer or Senior Software Developer? What place should Java occupy? Should I present myself primarily as a backend or full-stack developer? How could I incorporate artificial intelligence without making it appear more important than eighteen years of experience? Which jobs and projects best represented my current path?

These questions cannot be answered by modifying CSS. They require us to review our own history and decide which version of it we are prepared to uphold publicly.

In trying to explain to someone else who I am professionally, I first had to explain it to myself.

## Choosing also means giving something up

A long career contains too many experiences, technologies, and responsibilities to give them all equal weight.

Trying to include every element does not produce a more complete picture. It generally produces noise.

Presenting myself as a senior Java developer means that other technologies take a complementary role. Highlighting enterprise applications and integration means not centring the narrative on isolated frontend work.

Discussing AI-augmented development requires clarifying that direction, review, and responsibility remain human.

Showing portfolio projects requires distinguishing them from commercial or client work.

These choices force us to abandon the idea that a professional identity must contain every possible version of ourselves.

A portfolio does not answer exhaustively who I was. It attempts to show who I am now and what others can expect from me.

## Coherence does not appear by itself

Our professional channels often tell different stories.

A CV may present us one way and LinkedIn another. GitHub may be dominated by old repositories that no longer represent our level. A biography written years ago may still describe goals we no longer have.

While those elements remain separate, contradictions can go unnoticed. A portfolio brings them together.

If someone presents themselves as a senior backend developer while their visible repositories contain only basic exercises, there is an inconsistency.

If they claim to use artificial intelligence professionally but cannot explain how they use it or which responsibility they retain, there is another.

Building a portfolio forces us to examine these fractures and align how we present ourselves with what we can genuinely sustain.

## From claims to evidence

A CV consists mainly of claims:

Experience with Java. Knowledge of integration. Ability to develop full-stack applications. Professional use of artificial intelligence.

A portfolio can accompany those claims with evidence.

A project can show the problem, architecture, technologies, tests, development time, methodology, limitations, and repository.

An article can show how someone thinks about a technical or professional issue.

The site itself can become evidence of design, internationalisation, accessibility, SEO, deployment, and maintenance.

A good portfolio does not replace experience, but it narrows the distance between saying and demonstrating.

## It also shows what is missing

Creating a project section is not only about deciding what to show. It also reveals which capabilities still lack sufficient public evidence.

There may be genuine integration experience but no visible project that demonstrates it.

There may be knowledge of Spring Boot but no published and documented application.

Someone may have worked for years on enterprise systems without ever explaining the decisions that make them reliable.

That gap can become a roadmap.

Future projects stop being chosen only because they seem interesting. They begin to be chosen because they complete a professional picture:

one can demonstrate backend and persistence; another, integration and messaging; another, security and architecture; another, responsible use of AI.

A portfolio is therefore not only a record of past work. It can also organise future work.

## A professional memory

Over time, it is easy to forget how much we have done.

Projects end, companies change, technologies are replaced, and details disappear behind new responsibilities.

A portfolio preserves a selected memory:

what I built; which problems I solved; which technologies I used; what I learned; how I thought at the time; what changed afterwards.

It is not a neutral archive. Every presentation involves selection. Yet that selection makes it possible to recognise continuity where there once seemed to be isolated episodes.

It also reveals evolution. An old project can represent one stage; a new one can demonstrate a change in level, methodology, or ambition.

## It is not a finished work

There is a risk of treating a portfolio as a definitive work.

Trying to make everything perfect before publishing can turn it into an endless task. There will always be a missing project, translation, visual correction, or better description.

But a professional identity is not finished either.

A portfolio is therefore better understood as a living system. It is published when it represents the present with dignity and then evolves:

projects are added; inaccuracies are corrected; experience is updated; articles are published; elements that are no longer relevant are removed; the narrative is adjusted.

This evolution is not a flaw. It is part of its purpose.

A portfolio should not pretend that a career is complete. It should show that it has direction.

## So, who reads it?

It may be read by a recruiter who needs to understand a profile quickly.

It may be read by a technical decision-maker looking for signs of judgement and experience.

It may be read by a colleague, a potential client, or someone who arrived through an article.

It may also be read by almost nobody for a week.

But there is another reader who is always present: the person who builds it.

That reader uses the portfolio to review the past, organise the present, and project the future.

Its external usefulness depends on factors we do not completely control: traffic, opportunities, selection processes, and other people’s attention.

Its internal usefulness begins the moment we honestly try to answer:

What do I want to show, what can I demonstrate, and what do I still need to build?

## Building to be seen and to see oneself

A portfolio must be designed for others. It needs to be clear, accessible, and easy to explore. It cannot become a purely autobiographical exercise.

But it should not be measured only by the number of visits.

Building it can produce something that precedes any job opportunity: a more precise understanding of one’s professional position.

It forces us to find a thread between scattered experiences.

It turns capabilities into evidence.

It exposes contradictions.

It reveals gaps.

It organises future projects.

Perhaps many people will not read a complete portfolio.

Even so, it may be worth building.

Because we are not only creating a place for others to know us.

We are also building a place from which to recognise ourselves.
